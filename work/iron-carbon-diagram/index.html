Interactive Iron–Carbon Diagram

A dense metallurgy chart that you read by pointing at it. Hover over a phase, click for its full sheet, or cool a steel and watch which phases it passes through.

Where it came fromLink to this section#

I met the iron–carbon diagram in a materials-science course during my bachelor's, and I was struck by how much it packs into one picture. Every region is a phase, every line is a transformation, and every point is a precise composition and temperature at which the structure of the metal changes.

As a flat image on a textbook page, though, it is intimidating. You get a wall of overlapping zones, Greek letters (α, γ, δ) and invariant points, and you're expected to hold all of it in your head at once. Every time I looked at it I had the same thought: this is already a map, so why can't I just point at a place and have it tell me what's there?

The idea: read it by positionLink to this section#

That thought became the premise. The diagram's meaning is positional: where you sit on the carbon axis and how hot you are decide everything. So the main interaction had to be positional too. A student shouldn't have to memorise which zone is which. They should be able to move across the diagram and get exactly the information that belongs to the spot they're on.

The metallurgy comes from the textbooks. What I built is the interface: you point at a part of the chart and it tells you what is there.

What it doesLink to this section#

The first layer gives the quick answer. Move over any region and a small card appears with the name of the phase, its composition and temperature range, and a one-line description. There's nothing to click and no menu to open: the information shows up wherever your cursor is.

The second layer goes deeper. Click a region or an invariant point and a full study sheet opens next to the diagram. It covers the phases involved, their crystal structures, what physically happens on cooling, the mechanical behaviour that results and the typical applications. There are even small animations of the crystal lattices and of the transformation itself. Each region and each point has its own sheet, so what you read always matches where you clicked.

Following a cooling pathLink to this section#

The feature I cared about most answers the question every exercise ends up asking: if I take a steel with a given carbon content and cool it, what does it actually pass through before it becomes the final product? In the cooling explorer you pick a composition and press Cool down. A marker then moves down the temperature axis in real time and tells you which phase field you're in at each moment. Meanwhile the panel builds, from top to bottom, the full list of fields it crosses and the invariant reactions it triggers.

γ → α + Fe₃C

the eutectoid reaction at 727 °C, where austenite becomes pearlite

The diagram’s three invariant reactions sit on horizontal isotherms: the peritectic L + δ → γ at 1493 °C, the eutectic L → γ + Fe₃C at 1148 °C, and the eutectoid γ → α + Fe₃C at 727 °C, which is the one that governs every steel. The cooling path detects each of them at the exact temperature where it is crossed.

Checking the numbersLink to this section#

I built the diagram on real numbers: the eutectoid at 727 °C and 0.77 % C, the eutectic at 1148 °C and 4.30 % C, the peritectic at 1493 °C, and cementite fixed at 6.67 % C. Before trusting any of it, I checked the field boundaries against established references.

One decision I'm particularly happy with is the carbon axis. Almost everything students work on sits between 0 and about 2 % C, the steel range, yet that's a thin slice of a diagram that runs all the way to 6.67 %. So I broke the axis. The steel range takes most of the width and stays readable, the cast-iron range is compressed, and a small break glyph tells the reader that the scale changes there.

LimitsLink to this section#

It is a study aid to use next to the textbook, and it doesn't calculate phase fractions. It's still in active development, especially the visuals and images. The core interaction already works: you point at a region of the diagram and the panel tells you what is there.
